# Editorial style

Use this guide when writing or revising any chapter of *Medical Imaging: What Medical Imaging Reveals About Disease*.

## Voice and explanation

- Write in clear, direct English. Prefer familiar words and active voice; define specialized terms the first time they appear.
- Teach the reader what an image can show and how that finding answers a clinical question. Do not rely on lists of labels without explaining their meaning.
- Match the language to the section. Patient-facing material should explain the examination in accessible terms. Technical chapters may use specialist terminology, with enough context for a learner to follow it.
- Keep explanations specific to the modality, disease, and clinical setting. Remove generic introductions, repeated cautions, and boilerplate conclusions.
- Use measured language: distinguish a clue from a diagnosis, state relevant limitations, and avoid unsupported certainty or invented accuracy percentages.

## Clinical imaging entries

When a chapter catalogs diseases or findings for an imaging examination, explain each item in prose that answers:

1. What symptoms or signs prompt the examination? Label this paragraph **Symptoms:**.
2. Which image features should the reader inspect, and what do they suggest?
3. Is the examination usually primary, supplementary, or used for a specific task?
4. Can the image establish the diagnosis, or are clinical assessment, another modality, laboratory testing, or tissue sampling needed?

Keep these explanations condition-specific. If one part does not apply, say so naturally instead of repeating an empty template. In the expanded catalogue lists, place the condition name first, then put **Symptoms:**, **What to look for:**, and **Role and sufficiency:** on separate paragraph lines for easy scanning.

## Organization and terminology

- Use descriptive section headings and a logical progression from purpose and acquisition to findings, interpretation, and limitations.
- Expand an abbreviation at first use in each chapter unless it is universally familiar to the intended reader.
- Use the same name for the same modality, anatomy, measurement, or classification throughout the book.
- Keep tables for compact comparisons and reference data. Use prose or enumerated entries when the reader needs clinical reasoning or explanation.
- State when a finding is nonspecific, when a modality is not first-line, and when a negative study cannot exclude disease.

## Medical accuracy

- Describe findings as modality-dependent observations, not proof of a disease unless the test is genuinely definitive in that context.
- Separate established clinical uses from selected, supplementary, emerging, and investigational applications.
- Avoid fixed performance claims unless they are supported by a clearly applicable source and population.
- Include sources for claims that are disputed, unusually specific, or likely to change with practice guidance.
- Do not present educational text as individualized medical advice.
